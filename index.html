01

A nation of beekeepers

The European honey bee arrived in Australia in 1822. Today almost 49,000 people keep bees, from backyard hobbyists with a single box to commercial operators who truck hundreds of hives across the country to follow the flowering of eucalypts and crops.

New South Wales is the heart of Australian beekeeping: it holds 46% of all hives and 31% of beekeepers. Apart from the tiny ACT, Victoria packs the most hives into each square kilometre, while a typical NSW beekeeper runs more than twice as many hives as one in Victoria. Beekeeper numbers jumped everywhere after 2018 — in NSW they doubled as owners rushed to register when varroa mite was found in 2022.

A few professionals do most of the work

Just 1,872 commercial beekeepers (those with more than 50 hives) make up only 4% of all beekeepers, yet they own nearly three in every four hives.

Each grid has 100 squares. Each square is 1% of the total.

02

From hive to jar

How much honey Australia makes depends on the weather. Bees need flowering native trees, and drought or fire can wipe out a season's nectar in weeks.

−35%

The Black Summer collapse

In 2019-20 drought and the Black Summer bushfires burnt millions of hectares of forest that bees rely on. Commercial honey production fell to 14,949 tonnes — 35% below the 2012-13 peak.

The chart shows honey on which the national honey levy was paid — the best year-by-year measure of commercial production.

Less honey used to mean better prices

Follow the line from the pale dot (2011-12) to the dark dot (2022-23). As harvests shrank after 2014, the price paid to beekeepers climbed to $6.50/kg. Since 2020 that link has broken: prices fell to $5.00/kg even though harvests stayed about the same — while imports of cheap honey rose.

Each dot is one year. Right = more honey; up = higher price.

Our honey grows in public forests

Most commercial honey depends on access to state forests and national parks. In Tasmania 90% of honey production and hive build-up relies on public land; in Queensland and Western Australia, 80%. NSW alone has 8,500 apiary sites on public land.

Circle size = number of public-land apiary sites. Colour = share of production relying on public land.

We now buy more honey than we sell

Honey imports rose sharply after 2019, reaching 8,797 tonnes in 2022-23, four times what Australia exported. Most imports now come from Asia and cost less than $3 per kilogram on arrival.

Bars below the line are imports (by region of origin); bars above are exports.

03

Sweet trade

Australia exports only a small share of its honey, but it sells it far and wide — about 4,900 tonnes worth US$42 million in 2024, to more than 40 countries. Slide through the years to watch the buyers change.

The United States overtakes China

China was our biggest honey customer for most of the decade. Since 2022 the United States has led, and in 2024 it bought US$9.4 million of Australian honey — almost 14 times what it spent in 2016.

Darker cells = higher export value. Buyers are sorted by their 2015–2024 total.

Volume or value?

Not every buyer pays the same. The US takes the most honey but at around US$5.40/kg, largely bulk. China, Hong Kong and Vietnam pay three to five times more per kilogram, so a small volume earns a large share of export income.

Width = tonnes bought, height = price per kg. The area of each bar is the total value.

04

Bees feed the nation

Honey is only part of the story. Honey bees move pollen between flowers, which many fruit, nut, vegetable and seed crops need to set fruit. Crops worth $12.9 billion rely at least partly on honey bees, and bees add an estimated $4.6 billion to what those crops produce. That is more than ten times the value of the honey itself.

38–62%

Almonds alone need most of our hives

Almond trees cannot set nuts without bees. Australia's 60,463 ha of almond orchards need between 242,000 and 393,000 hives each spring — up to 62% of every commercial hive in the country, mostly trucked to the Murray Valley. Paid pollination earned beekeepers $99.2 million in 2023, up from $40.2 million in 2019.

Each bar spans the low and high estimate of hives needed. Dashed lines show how many hives exist.

05

The mite that changed everything

For decades Australia was the only inhabited continent free of Varroa destructor, a pinhead-sized parasite that feeds on bees and spreads viruses. Wherever it has spread overseas, most wild honey bee colonies have died out. In June 2022 it was found in sentinel hives at the Port of Newcastle.

27,000+

hives removed from eradication zones

NSW spent 14 months trying to wipe varroa out: more than 27,000 hives were removed from red eradication zones and $101 million was spent nationally. In September 2023 authorities agreed eradication was no longer possible and moved to managing the mite. Since then it has reached Victoria (2024), Queensland, the ACT and South Australia (2025), and in September 2026 it was found near Cairns — the most northerly detection yet. In 2026, mites in northern NSW also showed resistance to two common treatments.

Without wild bees doing free pollination, farmers will need more managed hives — and beekeepers will spend more time and money keeping their colonies alive.

What it all means

Small industry, big reachHoney is worth hundreds of millions of dollars, but the pollination it enables is worth billions and touches almost every fruit bowl.
Squeezed from two sidesWeather and fire limit how much honey we make, while cheap imports hold down the price beekeepers receive.
A new normalVarroa is here to stay. Registering hives, checking for mites and buying Australian honey all help keep the industry — and our food supply — healthy.
